# Atlas User-Friendly Product Script

## Short Version

Atlas is an AI travel concierge that helps you turn a trip idea into a polished, usable itinerary. You tell Atlas where you are going, when you are traveling, your budget, your group size, and what kind of trip you want. Atlas gives you a day-by-day plan with places to visit, restaurants, hotel ideas, transportation notes, a checklist, a map, calendar export, print options, and a shareable trip link.

The goal is simple: less time planning, more confidence that the trip actually makes sense.

## Demo Script

Today I am showing Atlas, an AI-powered travel planning app.

The problem Atlas solves is that travel planning is not just picking a few places. You have to think about dates, neighborhoods, restaurants, hotels, transport, timing, reservations, maps, budgets, and all the little tasks you need to do before leaving. Atlas brings those pieces together in one place.

To start, I enter where I want to go. Atlas supports one destination or multiple destination legs, so it can handle a simple weekend trip or a multi-city itinerary.

I also enter my travel dates. Dates are required because Atlas is designed to build a real itinerary, not just a generic list of ideas. The app uses those dates to organize each day and build the checklist around the actual trip window.

Next, I choose group size, budget, and trip style. If I want a looser plan, I can choose Flexible. If I want a detailed schedule, I can choose Structured. If I only want the best few recommendations, I can choose Top 3.

I can also add interests, must-dos, and loyalty programs. This helps Atlas tailor the itinerary instead of giving generic tourist recommendations.

Before Atlas generates the trip, it validates the destination. This matters because many places have the same name. If I type something ambiguous, Atlas can ask me which destination I mean instead of quietly planning the wrong trip.

Once I submit, Atlas starts building the trip. The main itinerary and the supporting trip details are generated in parallel, so the day-by-day plan can appear quickly while hotels, budget, transport, and checklist details continue loading.

When the results appear, the top of the page gives me the trip summary, dates, pace, stops, highlights, and checklist count.

Below that, I get an interactive map. For a normal itinerary, I can view all days together or focus on the selected day. For a Top 3 trip, the map shows the three overall picks without pretending they are one daily route.

Each activity includes the time, name, description, price range, and a link to open or search for the place. When Google Places can verify the location, Atlas shows a verified badge, rating, map link, and sometimes a photo. If Atlas cannot confidently verify a place, it avoids forcing a bad pin onto the map. That is intentional: fewer accurate pins are better than wrong pins.

The itinerary also includes Atlas tips, but they are limited. The idea is that tips should feel useful and specific, not like filler text on every item.

If I want to make a small change, I can use Swap on a single activity. For example, I can ask for something cheaper, more casual, more outdoorsy, or better for a group.

If I want to redo a whole day, I can use Regenerate day. Atlas keeps the same date and destination but gives me a fresh set of ideas.

Below the itinerary, Atlas gives me the practical trip support: hotel recommendation, transportation overview, budget breakdown, and a pre-trip checklist.

The checklist is one of the most useful parts because it turns the itinerary into action. It reminds me what I still need to book or prepare, grouped by category, with priority and timing labels.

Once I like the trip, Atlas saves it automatically and creates a short share link. I can copy that link and send it to someone else. I can also reopen saved trips from My Trips.

If I make edits to my saved trip, those edits are saved too. So when I reopen the share link or open it from My Trips, I see the updated version, not the original draft.

Atlas also supports calendar export. I can download an ICS file and add the itinerary to my calendar.

For printing, Atlas has a Print Pack option. I can print all days, one selected day, or a day range. The print version includes the trip details and checklist in a cleaner travel document rather than just printing the website.

In short, Atlas is not just a chatbot that gives travel ideas. It is a travel planning workspace. It generates the plan, validates the destination, maps the places, enriches verified locations, supports edits, saves trips, shares links, prints cleanly, and exports to calendar.

## Suggested One-Minute Pitch

Atlas is an AI travel concierge that turns a trip idea into a complete itinerary. You enter your destination, dates, budget, group size, and interests. Atlas validates the destination, builds a day-by-day plan, maps the activities, verifies places through Google Places, adds ratings and photos when available, and creates practical support like hotel ideas, transport notes, a budget breakdown, and a pre-trip checklist.

The user can edit the trip by swapping one activity, regenerating a day, or asking for broader changes. Once the trip is ready, Atlas saves it, creates a shareable link, exports to calendar, and produces a cleaner print version. The main value is that Atlas turns messy travel planning into one organized, editable, shareable workspace.

## Suggested Two-Minute Pitch

Atlas is built around a common travel problem: planning a trip requires a lot of fragmented work. You might search maps, blogs, restaurants, hotels, transit, reservation rules, and calendars, then manually stitch everything together.

Atlas brings that workflow into one app.

The user starts by entering destination, dates, group size, budget, trip style, interests, and optional loyalty programs. Atlas supports both single-destination and multi-city trips. Before generating, it validates the destination with Google Places so ambiguous inputs do not turn into wrong-location itineraries.

Then Atlas generates the trip in two parallel parts. One model call builds the day-by-day itinerary. Another builds the supporting details, like hotel recommendations, transportation, budget, tips, and the checklist. This makes the experience faster because the main itinerary can appear while supporting details continue loading.

After the itinerary renders, Atlas enriches places using Google Places. It can add verified map links, ratings, coordinates, and photos. It also rejects questionable map matches, because showing a wrong pin is worse than showing fewer pins.

The result is a polished trip page with a map, day cards, highlights, verified places, sparse tips, a practical checklist, save and share links, print options, and calendar export.

The user can keep editing after generation. They can swap one activity, regenerate an entire day, or regenerate Top 3 picks. Saved trips remember those edits, so reopening the trip later shows the updated version.

Atlas is designed to feel like a practical travel planning workspace, not just a chatbot. It helps users go from "I want to visit this place" to "Here is the plan, here is the map, here is what to book, and here is a link I can share."
